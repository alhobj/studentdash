Change a data set

Try a fixed scenario

Find the median of 2, 4, 4, 5, 20.

Worked explanation

The ordered list has five values. The third is 4. The mean is 7, which is more affected by the large value.

Hands-on explorer · SL 4.4

Watch an outlier change regression

Start with: SL 4.4 small steps

Move the final y-value in a five-point data set. Compare the least-squares line and Pearson's r. Association alone is not evidence of causation.

Try a fixed scenario

A fitted model is y = 2x + 1. What is its predicted y-value at x = 4?

Worked explanation

y = 2(4) + 1 = 9. Predictions far outside the observed x-range may be unreliable.

Experiment with a binomial model

Start with: SL 4.5 small steps · SL 4.8 small steps

Change n and p, then simulate batches of independent trials with the same success probability. Bars show exact probabilities; the simulation shows sampling variability.

Booklet help: formulas and data

Related references for this section. Use values and assumptions stated in the question.

Event relationships

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B); P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B)

Check independence before multiplying.

Discrete distributions

E(X) = ΣxP(X = x); binomial mean = np, variance = np(1 − p)

For the binomial model, n counts trials and p is success probability. Trials must be independent with the same p.

Try a fixed scenario

For three independent fair coin tosses, what is P(exactly two heads)?

Worked explanation

There are three ways to get two heads, each with probability (0.5)³. The total is 3/8 = 0.375.

Hands-on explorer · SL 4.9

Move a normal-distribution cutoff

Start with: SL 4.9 small steps

Adjust a mean, standard deviation and cutoff. The shaded area estimates P(X ≤ cutoff). This is a continuous model; the probability at one exact point is zero.

Try a fixed scenario

For any normal distribution, what is P(X ≤ the mean)?

Worked explanation

The normal density is symmetric about its mean, with half of its total area on either side.

Hands-on explorer · SL 4.1

Audit a sampling method

Start with: SL 4.1 small steps

A fictional population has 60 younger and 40 older members, with group mean scores 40 and 70. Compare expected sample composition across methods; this is a design comparison, not random data generation.

Try a fixed scenario

In a proportional sample of 20 from a population that is 40% older, how many older members should be sampled?

Worked explanation

20 × 0.4 = 8. Stratified sampling randomly samples within groups; filling quotas by convenience can still introduce bias.

Try a fixed scenario

For ordered data 1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8 using medians of the lower and upper halves, find the interquartile range.

Worked explanation

Q1=2.5 and Q3=6.5, so IQR=4. Outlier fences are Q1−1.5IQR and Q3+1.5IQR. A valid unusual observation should not automatically be deleted.

Event relationships

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B); P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B)

Check independence before multiplying.

Try a fixed scenario

A bag contains 3 red and 2 blue counters. Draw twice without replacement. Find P(two red).

Worked explanation

P(RR) = 3/5 × 2/4 = 0.3. The second probability changes because the first counter is not replaced.

Hands-on explorer · SL 4.7

Price a discrete game fairly

Start with: SL 4.7 small steps

Win 0, 5 or 20 credits. Change the probability of the top prize and entry fee. The 5-credit outcome has probability 0.30; the zero prize takes the remaining probability.

Discrete distributions

E(X) = ΣxP(X = x); binomial mean = np, variance = np(1 − p)

For the binomial model, n counts trials and p is success probability. Trials must be independent with the same p.

Practise: Read a probability distribution

Try a fixed scenario

Prizes 0, 5, 20 have probabilities 0.6, 0.3, 0.1. Find a fair entry fee.

Worked explanation

E(prize)=0(0.6)+5(0.3)+20(0.1)=3.5. A fee of 3.5 gives expected net gain zero, not a guaranteed zero gain on each play.

Hands-on explorer · SL 4.9

Find a percentile and an interval

Start with: SL 4.9 small steps

Set a normal mean and standard deviation and move the cumulative percentile. The cutoff is found numerically. Compare the probability within one standard deviation of the mean.

Try a fixed scenario

For a normal distribution with mean 100, what is its 50th percentile?

Worked explanation

The median equals the mean by symmetry. Inverse normal calculations start with an area and find its boundary value.

Hands-on explorer · SL 4.10

Rank data with tied values

Start with: SL 4.10 small steps

Change the final observation in the pairs (1,1),(2,2),(3,2),(4,4),(5,y). Compare Pearson correlation with correlation of the average ranks. Tied ranks are averaged.

Try a fixed scenario

Two observations occupy rank positions 2 and 3 and are tied. What rank is assigned to each?

Worked explanation

Each receives (2+3)/2 = 2.5. Pearson measures linear association; Spearman measures monotonic association and is generally less sensitive to extreme magnitudes.

Hands-on explorer · SL 4.11

Test categorical data with chi-square

Start with: SL 4.11 small steps

Compare observations with expectations. Independence mode uses two groups and three choices (df=2); goodness-of-fit mode uses three equally likely outcomes (df=2). These teaching tables keep every expected count above 5.

Try a fixed scenario

For a 2-by-3 contingency table, what are the degrees of freedom?

Worked explanation

df=(2−1)(3−1)=2. For goodness of fit to three specified category probabilities, df=3−1=2. Compare p to α; failing to reject is not proof of H₀.

Hands-on explorer · SL 4.11

Compare two independent means

Start with: SL 4.11 small steps

Use the pooled two-sample t-test for independent samples from normal populations with unknown but equal variances. Both sample sizes are 10 and group B's mean is 50. Choose the alternative before interpreting p.

Try a fixed scenario

Two independent samples each have size 10. Find the pooled t-test degrees of freedom.

Worked explanation

df=nA+nB−2=18. H₀ states equal population means; the alternative determines which tail or tails contribute to p.

Summarizing data

IQR = Q₃ − Q₁; mean = Σ(fx)/Σf

Use frequencies as weights.

Try a fixed scenario

A data set has mean 7 and population variance 16. Each value is multiplied by 3 and then increased by 2. Find the new variance.

Worked explanation

Multiplication by 3 multiplies variance by 3²; adding a constant does not change spread. New variance = 9 × 16 = 144; new mean = 23.
