R3.1 · Start here

Transfer a proton

A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates a proton (H⁺); a base accepts one.

Booklet help: formulas and data

Related references for this section. Use values and assumptions stated in the question.

Acid–base calculations

pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; K_w = [H⁺][OH⁻]

Use the concentration after any dilution or reaction. K_w depends on temperature; acid strength and concentration are different ideas.

Constants

Nₐ ≈ 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹; R ≈ 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹

Match the required precision.

Indicator ranges

Match the titration jump.

Step 1

Give me a hint

Compare HCl with Cl⁻.

Show the worked step

Each HCl donates one H⁺.

Step 2

Give me a hint

Accepting a proton is the role of a base.

Show the worked step

Water is acting as a Brønsted–Lowry base.

Practise more like this

Identify the role of a proton acceptor.

6 closely matched questions. Keep using the same method. There is no timer or score; you can repeat the set.

Question 1 of 6

Give me a hint

A Brønsted–Lowry base accepts a proton.

Show the worked answer

H₂O is acting as a base because it accepts H⁺.

Read or print all 6 questions and solutions

Question 1

In H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺, H₂O accepts a proton. Is it acting as an acid or a base?

Worked answer

H₂O is acting as a base because it accepts H⁺.

Question 2

In NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺, NH₃ accepts a proton. Is it acting as an acid or a base?

Worked answer

NH₃ is acting as a base because it accepts H⁺.

Question 3

In OH⁻ + H⁺ → H₂O, OH⁻ accepts a proton. Is it acting as an acid or a base?

Worked answer

OH⁻ is acting as a base because it accepts H⁺.

Question 4

In CO₃²⁻ + H⁺ → HCO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻ accepts a proton. Is it acting as an acid or a base?

Worked answer

CO₃²⁻ is acting as a base because it accepts H⁺.

Question 5

In F⁻ + H⁺ → HF, F⁻ accepts a proton. Is it acting as an acid or a base?

Worked answer

F⁻ is a Brønsted–Lowry base because it accepts H⁺.

Question 6

In HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → H₂CO₃, HCO₃⁻ accepts a proton. Is it acting as an acid or a base?

Worked answer

HCO₃⁻ is acting as a base by accepting a proton.

R3.2 · Start here

Transfer electrons

Oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is gain of electrons.

Booklet help: formulas and data

Related references for this section. Use values and assumptions stated in the question.

Electrode potentials

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

Subtract tabulated reduction potentials. Do not multiply voltages by balancing coefficients. Quantitative cell-potential work is AHL.

Constants

Nₐ ≈ 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹; R ≈ 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹

Match the required precision.

Step 1

Give me a hint

Balance the +2 charge with negative electrons.

Show the worked step

Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻.

Step 2

Give me a hint

Use oxidation = loss.

Show the worked step

Magnesium undergoes oxidation.

Practise more like this

Count electrons lost when a positive ion forms.

6 closely matched questions. Keep using the same method. There is no timer or score; you can repeat the set.

Question 1 of 6

Give me a hint

Balance the positive charge with negative electrons on the right.

Show the worked answer

Na → Na⁺ + 1e⁻. Loss of electrons is oxidation.

Read or print all 6 questions and solutions

Question 1

Complete Na → Na⁺ + __ e⁻. Enter the electron count.

Worked answer

Na → Na⁺ + 1e⁻. Loss of electrons is oxidation.

Question 2

Complete Ca → Ca²⁺ + __ e⁻. Enter the electron count.

Worked answer

Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻. Loss of electrons is oxidation.

Question 3

Complete Al → Al³⁺ + __ e⁻. Enter the electron count.

Worked answer

Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻. Loss of electrons is oxidation.

Question 4

Complete Zn → Zn²⁺ + __ e⁻. Enter the electron count.

Worked answer

Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. Loss of electrons is oxidation.

Question 5

Complete K → K⁺ + __ e⁻. Enter the electron count.

Worked answer

K loses one electron: K → K⁺ + e⁻.

Question 6

Complete Ba → Ba²⁺ + __ e⁻. Enter the electron count.

Worked answer

Ba loses two electrons: Ba → Ba²⁺ + 2e⁻.

R3.3 · Start here

Split a bond equally

In homolytic bond breaking, each atom receives one electron from the shared pair. A radical has an unpaired electron.

Booklet help: formulas and data

Related references for this section. Use values and assumptions stated in the question.

Bond energies

ΔH ≈ Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed)

Count every bond.

Step 1

Give me a hint

Share the two bonding electrons equally.

Show the worked step

Each chlorine atom receives one electron from the bond.

Step 2

Give me a hint

Each of the two chlorine atoms becomes a radical.

Show the worked step

Cl₂ → 2Cl•. Two chlorine radicals form.

Practise more like this

Count radicals from homolytic bond breaking.

6 closely matched questions. Keep using the same method. There is no timer or score; you can repeat the set.

Question 1 of 6

Give me a hint

Every split molecule produces two radicals.

Show the worked answer

1 × 2 = 2 radicals.

Read or print all 6 questions and solutions

Question 1

1 Br₂ molecule(s) each split homolytically into two radicals. How many radicals form?

Worked answer

1 × 2 = 2 radicals.

Question 2

2 Cl₂ molecule(s) each split homolytically into two radicals. How many radicals form?

Worked answer

2 × 2 = 4 radicals.

Question 3

3 Br₂ molecule(s) each split homolytically into two radicals. How many radicals form?

Worked answer

3 × 2 = 6 radicals.

Question 4

4 Cl₂ molecule(s) each split homolytically into two radicals. How many radicals form?

Worked answer

4 × 2 = 8 radicals.

Question 5

5 Cl₂ molecules each split homolytically into two radicals. How many radicals form?

Worked answer

5 × 2 = 10 Cl radicals.

Question 6

6 Br₂ molecules each split homolytically into two radicals. How many radicals form?

Worked answer

6 × 2 = 12 Br radicals.

R3.4 · Start here

Donate an electron pair

A nucleophile donates an electron pair to an electron-deficient centre. In a mechanism, a full curly arrow shows movement of a pair.

Step 1

Give me a hint

A full curly arrow moves one pair, not one electron.

Show the worked step

A full curly arrow represents movement of 2 electrons.

Step 2

Give me a hint

Use the definition above.

Show the worked step

A nucleophile donates an electron pair; an electrophile accepts it.

Practise more like this

Count electrons in one donated lone pair.

6 closely matched questions. Keep using the same method. There is no timer or score; you can repeat the set.

Question 1 of 6

Give me a hint

A lone pair contains two electrons.

Show the worked answer

OH⁻ donates a pair of 2 electrons. A full curly arrow represents movement of that pair.

Read or print all 6 questions and solutions

Question 1

In a model reaction, OH⁻ donates one lone pair to form a bond. How many electrons are in the donated pair?

Worked answer

OH⁻ donates a pair of 2 electrons. A full curly arrow represents movement of that pair.

Question 2

In a model reaction, NH₃ donates one lone pair to form a bond. How many electrons are in the donated pair?

Worked answer

NH₃ donates a pair of 2 electrons. A full curly arrow represents movement of that pair.

Question 3

In a model reaction, H₂O donates one lone pair to form a bond. How many electrons are in the donated pair?

Worked answer

H₂O donates a pair of 2 electrons. A full curly arrow represents movement of that pair.

Question 4

In a model reaction, Cl⁻ donates one lone pair to form a bond. How many electrons are in the donated pair?

Worked answer

Cl⁻ donates a pair of 2 electrons. A full curly arrow represents movement of that pair.

Question 5

In a model reaction, Br⁻ donates one lone pair to form a bond. How many electrons are in the donated pair?

Worked answer

A lone pair contains two electrons; Br⁻ donates that pair.

Question 6

In a model reaction, I⁻ donates one lone pair to form a bond. How many electrons are in the donated pair?

Worked answer

I⁻ donates a pair of two electrons, represented by a full curly arrow.
