01 / Mole ratios

Which reactant runs out?

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Model a reaction proceeding to completion. Use amounts in moles, not masses. Before changing a slider, predict which reactant is limiting and how much water can form.

Try increasing only the excess reactant. Does the theoretical yield change?

All bars use the same 0–10 mol scale.

Method and worked example

Divide each initial amount by its coefficient: n(H₂)/2 and n(O₂)/1. The smaller value sets the reaction extent. Multiply this by 2 to find n(H₂O).

With 4.0 mol H₂ and 3.0 mol O₂, the extent is min(2.0, 3.0) = 2.0 mol. H₂ is limiting; 4.0 mol H₂O forms and 1.0 mol O₂ remains. Equal ratios mean neither reactant is in excess.

Check your reasoning

For 3.0 mol H₂ and 2.0 mol O₂, what is the theoretical amount of water formed?

02 / Concentration

Same solute, more solution

c₁V₁ = c₂V₂

Keep the amount of dissolved solute constant while increasing the final solution volume. This model assumes no reaction or loss of solute. V₂ is the total final volume, not the volume of water added.

Colour illustrates relative concentration only; the vessel is not a volume scale.

Method and worked example

First find the amount: n = c₁ × V₁/1000 when V₁ is in cm³. Then c₂ = n / (V₂/1000). Alternatively, c₂ = c₁V₁/V₂ using matching volume units.

25.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ solution contains 0.00500 mol. Diluting to 100.0 cm³ gives 0.0500 mol dm⁻³. The amount of solute has not changed.

Check your reasoning

10.0 cm³ of 0.300 mol dm⁻³ solution is diluted to 150.0 cm³. Calculate the final concentration.

03 / Reaction direction

Compare Q with K

H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g)

At a fixed temperature, use Qc = [HI]² / ([H₂][I₂]). Predict the direction of net reaction from the current mixture. The chosen Kc is illustrative, not a measured value at a specified temperature.

Reaction quotient

Q < KQ = KQ > K

The marker uses log₁₀(Q/K), clipped at ±2. It shows relative position, not reaction speed or an equilibrium composition.

Method and worked example

Q < K: net forward reaction increases Q. Q > K: net reverse reaction decreases Q. Q = K: the mixture is at dynamic equilibrium; both reactions continue at equal rates.

With [H₂] = [I₂] = [HI] = 1.00 mol dm⁻³, Q = 1.00. For K = 4.00, the net reaction is forward. Raising [HI] to 2.00 gives Q = 4.00, so the mixture is at equilibrium.

Changing concentrations does not change K at fixed temperature. Selecting a different K represents a different temperature scenario; this activity does not calculate the temperature dependence of K.

04 / Bonding and structure

Connect particles to properties

Choose a substance and state, then predict whether it conducts electricity. Use the particles and their mobility to explain your answer.

Schematic only, not to scale. Positions are illustrative; the diagram does not simulate motion.

Compare the three bonding models
  • Ionic: electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions throughout a lattice. Solid NaCl has fixed ions; molten NaCl has mobile ions.
  • Covalent: electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and both bonded nuclei. Water contains molecules; diamond and graphite form extended covalent structures.
  • Metallic: electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalized electrons. Mobile electrons allow conduction in solid and molten copper.

Melting ice overcomes intermolecular attractions; it does not break the O–H covalent bonds within each water molecule. Graphite conducts along its layers because some electrons are delocalized. Diamond has no comparable mobile charge carriers. Pure water has very low conductivity due to its slight self-ionization.

Explain: Why is “all covalent substances have low melting points and cannot conduct” incorrect? Use diamond and graphite as counterexamples.

05 / Structure 1.2 · The nuclear atom

Read an atom or ion

Select a species. Predict its proton, neutron and electron counts, then reveal the nuclear model. Mass number A is a whole-number nucleon count, not relative atomic mass.

How to find the counts

Protons = Z. Neutrons = A − Z. Electrons = Z − charge, where the charge is signed. For ²³Na⁺, Z = 11 and A = 23: 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 10 electrons.

Protons (+1) and neutrons (0) are in a tiny, dense nucleus containing almost all the atom's mass. Electrons (−1) occupy the surrounding region and have much smaller mass. The atom is mostly empty space; any schematic nucleus is enlarged for readability.

Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Forming an ion changes the electron count, not the nucleus.

Find relative atomic mass

Model a hypothetical sample containing only carbon-12 and carbon-13. Use 12 and 13 as approximate isotope masses. Change the abundance and predict the weighted mean. These slider values are not a claim about natural abundance.

Relative atomic mass is dimensionless. An average such as 12.25 does not mean that an individual nucleus contains a fractional neutron.

06 / Electron configurations

Fill subshells and orbitals

Practise selected ground-state atoms and ions, including chromium and copper. Enter subshell notation with spaces, for example 1s2 2s2 2p6 or [Ne]. Carets and superscript digits are also accepted. The checker accepts different subshell ordering, but requires the correct occupancies.

Boxes represent orbitals, not paths through space. Arrows represent opposite spin states. Diagram order is a filling guide, not a quantitative energy scale.

Rules and common traps
  • An orbital holds at most two electrons with opposite spins (Pauli exclusion principle).
  • Equal-energy orbitals fill singly with parallel spins before pairing (Hund's rule). An s subshell has 1 orbital, p has 3 and d has 5.
  • A useful initial filling order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d. For the selected neutral atoms, remember Cr is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
  • For the transition-metal cations here, remove 4s electrons before 3d electrons. Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶; Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵.

Check the electron total against atomic number and charge. The same electron count alone does not prove a correct configuration.

Explain: Compare the unpaired electrons of N and O, then compare Fe, Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺. Why can simply filling orbitals for the ion's electron count give a wrong transition-metal configuration?

07 / Mole · mass and amount

Same mass, different amount

Explore n = m/M. Compare equal masses of different substances by changing the molar mass. M is supplied in g mol⁻¹; it is not the mass of one molecule.

Worked example

For 11.0 g CO₂ with M = 44.0 g mol⁻¹, n = 11.0/44.0 = 0.250 mol. Doubling the mass doubles n; doubling M at fixed mass halves n.

08 / Mole · counting entities

Molecules are not atoms

Use N = nNₐ, with Nₐ = 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. Set the number of atoms in each molecule to compare molecule count with total atom count. This activity concerns molecular substances; ionic solids are counted using formula units.

Worked example

0.500 mol H₂O contains 3.01107038 × 10²³ molecules and three times as many atoms: 9.03321114 × 10²³. There are twice as many hydrogen atoms as oxygen atoms.

09 / Mole · empirical formulas

Turn masses into an atom ratio

Select an invented analysis. Divide each mass by its supplied molar mass, then divide by the smallest amount. If needed, multiply every ratio by the same small integer.

Why not use the mass ratio?

Atoms of different elements have different masses. Chemical formulas describe numbers of atoms, so compare amounts in moles. A ratio of 1:1.5 becomes 2:3, not 1:2. An empirical formula gives the simplest ratio; it may differ from a molecular formula.

10 / Ideal gases · pV = nRT

Control a gas sample

Predict how pressure changes when you change one variable. Use R = 8.314 kPa dm³ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, volume in dm³ and T = °C + 273.15. The model assumes negligible particle volume and no intermolecular forces.

Worked example and model limit

For 0.100 mol at 300.0 K in 2.00 dm³, p = 0.100 × 8.314 × 300.0 / 2.00 = 124.71 kPa. At fixed n and T, doubling V halves p. Real gases generally behave more ideally at low pressure and high temperature, away from condensation. The calculator does not model deviations.

11 / Ideal gases · changing conditions

Predict the new volume

A fixed amount of ideal gas changes pressure and temperature. Use p₁V₁/T₁ = p₂V₂/T₂. Temperatures below are in kelvin; use absolute pressure, not gauge pressure. No gas enters or leaves.

Worked example

A 2.00 dm³ sample at 100 kPa and 300 K changes to 200 kPa and 450 K. V₂ = 2.00 × (100/200) × (450/300) = 1.50 dm³. Heating and compression act in opposite directions; include both factors.

12 / Ideal gases · identifying a gas

Find molar mass from measurements

Find n = pV/RT, then M = m/n. Use the measured mass of gas alone. Inputs are absolute pressure in kPa, volume in dm³, temperature in K and mass in g.

Worked example

A 0.880 g gas sample occupies 0.500 dm³ at 100.0 kPa and 300.0 K. n = 50.0/2494.2 = 0.02005 mol, giving M ≈ 43.9 g mol⁻¹. A molar mass alone does not uniquely identify a gas. Measurement uncertainty and deviations from ideal behavior affect the estimate.

13 / Structure 2.4 · bonding continuum

Place a pair in the bonding triangle

Change two illustrative electronegativities, χA and χB. Plot their mean horizontally and their absolute difference vertically. Treat ionic, covalent and metallic bonding as models along a continuum, not rigid boxes.

Keep the mean fixed: increase one value while decreasing the other. What changes?

Schematic endpoints span 0–4, not a complete periodic-table scale. No sharp boundaries or percentages of bond type are inferred. Electronegativity alone cannot determine every material property.

14 / Structure 2.4 · alloys

Disturb a regular metal lattice

Compare a pure metal with schematic substitutional and interstitial alloys. Apply a notional shear to explore how other atoms can obstruct layer movement. This is a qualitative model, not a calculation of strength.

From model to material

An alloy contains a metal with other elements, which need not all be metals. Brass contains copper and zinc; carbon is important in steel. Alloying can change hardness, corrosion resistance and other properties. The outcome depends on composition and processing; not every alloy is harder than every pure metal.

15 / Structure 2.4 · addition polymers

Build a repeat unit from an alkene

Select a monomer and predict the repeat unit. In these addition reactions the C=C becomes a C–C link in the chain; side groups remain attached. No small molecule is eliminated.

Reading the chain

Brackets with n describe repeated units; bonds extend through the brackets to adjacent units. Actual chains can contain many more units than this display. Polymer properties also depend on chain length, branching, packing and interactions between chains; the repeat unit alone does not determine every property.

16 / Structure 2.4 · condensation extension

Join functional groups into a chain

Compare a diol + dicarboxylic acid with a diamine + dicarboxylic acid. R and R′ represent the remaining carbon-containing groups. These examples use carboxylic acids, so each new ester or amide link eliminates one water molecule.

Count individual molecules, not monomer pairs. The chain is open, unbranched and has no rings; only the links joining those molecules are counted. Hydrolysis can break ester/amide links by adding water. Other condensation routes can eliminate other small molecules.
